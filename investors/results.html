Results Measured 24 October 2026

What the tests show

We put the swarm through five situations, from perfect radio to none at all. Losing the radio made the swarm slower, but it never stopped defending.

How we tested

  1. Five situationsGood radio, patchy radio, a jammed area, the leader destroyed twice, and no radio at all.
  2. Ten runs eachEach run starts differently at random, so one lucky run can't skew the result.
  3. Two minutes per runA wave of 10 threats every 10 seconds: about 1,150 threats per situation.
  4. Count what happenedThreats stopped, threats that reached the site, and how fast a drone took each one on.

What this shows

  • The fallback works. With the leader destroyed twice in every run, not one threat got through.
  • Losing radio costs speed, not the defence. With every message lost, 94% of threats were still stopped.
  • The swarm heals itself. No person stepped in during any run: leaders were replaced and modes changed on their own.

What it doesn't show yet

  • Real hardware. This is a simulation of a flat, 2D world with simplified flight.
  • Telling threats apart. Drones are told which threat is which. Real sensors have to work that out.
  • Smart targeting. Simple hand-written rules pick targets; the trained AI is not plugged in yet.
  • Real interception. "Stopped" means a drone came within 12 metres of the threat.
For technical readersHow to reproduce these numbers

The five situations are the simulator's built-in presets (Clear skies, Patchy links, Interference zone, Coordinator lost, Total blackout) with 8 drones and a ground station. Each was run with random seeds 1 to 10 for 120 simulated seconds at 60 steps per second. Reaction time is measured from the moment a threat is first seen to the moment a drone targets it; a breach is a threat reaching the protected site.

From the repository root, run node investors/tools/bench.mjs to repeat the measurement.
