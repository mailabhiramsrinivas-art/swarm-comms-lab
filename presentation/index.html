1The problem

Defending against a swarm takes teamwork, and teamwork runs on radio

Attackers come as a group

Enemy drones arrive together, often in a formation where the outer drones protect the inner ones. Each defender must know which target is its job.

Splitting the work needs radio

To divide the targets, drones share what they see and who is taking what, over radio links between them.

Radio is the weak point

Links get jammed, fade with distance, or the station giving orders is destroyed. A swarm that freezes without its radio is no defence at all.

So we built a model that decides well and keeps talking, whatever happens to the radio.

Our model ↓
2Our model

One team, two layers

Every drone runs the same software. Its two layers work together: one decides, the other makes sure every drone hears the decision.

Decision layer

Decides who goes where

  • Reads an enemy formation: which threats can be hit now, and which are shielded.
  • Picks the smallest team that wins in time, and where each drone starts.
  • Each round, matches drones to threats so no strike is wasted.
⇄
Communication layer

Makes sure every drone hears it

  • Carries sightings, plans and claims between drones.
  • Keeps the team coordinated as links get jammed or fade.
  • Replaces a lost leader automatically, in about a second.

One attack, four steps

  1. SpotA drone sees the threat. The warning hops from drone to drone until the whole team knows.
  2. DecideOur model reads the formation and picks the team, and where each drone starts.
  3. ShareThe plan reaches every chosen drone by radio, five times a second.
  4. StrikeDrones that can reach the same threat agree who takes it, then strike together, round after round.

Next, a closer look at how the drones talk to each other.

How it talks ↓
3How it talks

Three kinds of short radio message

Drones talk many times a second. Every message is one of three kinds, shown in the same colours as the demonstration. Most are under 20 bytes, small enough to get through weak links.

Sighting

"I see a threat here"

Each drone shares what its own camera sees. Messages hop from drone to drone, so a warning reaches drones far out of direct range.

Plan

"You take this one"

The leader turns our model's decision into a job for every drone and sends it five times a second. The ground station leads whenever drones can hear it.

Claim

"I'll take this one"

Drones that can reach the same threat agree among themselves who takes it, so two drones never strike the same one.

When the radio gets worse, the team adapts on its own

Each drone checks what it can hear many times a second and moves back up to a better mode as soon as the radio allows. Nobody has to switch it.

Built to keep working

No fixed roles

Any drone can lead. The leader is simply the lowest-numbered one in contact, with the ground station always first in line.

No voting

Every drone applies the same rule to the same information, so they agree without sending extra messages.

Share what you see

Drones pass on raw sightings, not opinions, so every drone builds the same picture of the sky.

Act first, fix later

A drone commits to a target straight away. Two drones briefly chasing one threat costs less than everyone hesitating.

Flying stays on board

Each drone steers itself. The radio carries only decisions, so a lost message costs time, never control of the drone.

The result

No single drone or station is essential. Lose any one of them and the rest keep defending.

For technical readersAlgorithms, messages and timings

Assignment

The leader computes a globally optimal drone-to-threat assignment with the Hungarian algorithm and broadcasts it as a PLAN at 5 Hz. Without a plan, drones send CLAIMs with a cost; the lowest cost wins and ties go to the lower ID, so every drone resolves conflicts identically without another round of messages.

Leader election

The coordinator is the lowest ID heard recently. The ground station is ID 0. When it is silent past the leader timeout (1.0 s) the next lowest ID takes over; when a lower ID reappears, the stand-in steps down.

    How it decides ↓
    5How it decides

    Taking a formation apart, from the outside in

    Our model's decision layer was trained on a 3D formation game that captures the heart of the problem: an enemy formation protects its inner drones, so the defenders have to read it and peel it layer by layer.

    The game in plain words

    • An enemy formation. Between 4 and 20 enemy drones hold a shape in 3D space. They flee from our drones while trying to stay together.
    • Only the outside can be hit. Enemies deep inside are shielded by the ones around them, so the formation has to be peeled from the outside in.
    • Five rounds, fewest drones. Our team has five rounds to remove every enemy. Using fewer drones scores better, so sending too many is a cost, not a safe choice.

    Three decisions, every engagement

    Decision 1

    Read the shape

    It works out which enemies are exposed on the outside and which are shielded inside. That decides who can be hit right now.

    Decision 2

    Choose the team and place it

    It estimates its chance of winning with 1, 2, 3 or more drones and picks the smallest team with at least a 90% chance. Then it decides where each drone starts.

    Decision 3

    Peel from the outside in

    Each round it moves its drones and decides who hits whom, so no two drones waste a strike on the same enemy. As the outer layer falls, the inner enemies become exposed.

    A real example from the test set: 14 enemies held a formation with 4 shielded inside. Our model chose 6 drones and cleared the whole formation in 3 rounds.

    How it learned

    1. Copy an expertA slow planning program searched thousands of possible futures to find good moves. Our model learned by imitating it.
    2. PractiseIt then improved by trial and error, over 20,400 practice games.
    3. Decide on its ownIn use, the planner is gone. Our model makes every decision itself, fast enough to run in a web browser.

    What this shows

    • The fallback works. With the leader destroyed twice in every run, not one threat got through.
    • Losing radio costs speed, not the defence. With every message lost, 94% of threats were still stopped.
    • The team heals itself. No person stepped in during any run: leaders were replaced and modes changed on their own.
    • Our model reads formations. It sizes its response to the shape in front of it, and runs fast enough for a web browser.
    For technical readersHow to reproduce these numbers

    The five situations are the simulator's built-in presets (Clear skies, Patchy links, Interference zone, Coordinator lost, Total blackout) with 8 drones and a ground station. Each was run with random seeds 1 to 10 for 120 simulated seconds at 60 steps per second. Reaction time is measured from the moment a threat is first seen to the moment a drone targets it; a breach is a threat reaching the protected site.

    From the repository root, run node presentation/tools/bench.mjs to repeat the measurement. Decision-layer figures come from the formation game's final evaluation: 850 test formations, five games each.
