Taking a formation apart

Fourteen players hold a 3D formation. A learned team reads its shape, decides it needs six players, picks where to stand and removes everyone in three ticks. This page shows how, step by step, and lets you build a formation for it to counter.

The game in one paragraph

O is a formation of 4 to 20 players in open 3D space. D is a team of attackers that chooses its own size. Both sides see everything. Each tick, every player moves a little: D up to half a unit, O up to 0.4, with O fleeing the nearest attacker while keeping the formation together. Then each D player may hit one O player within one unit of it. One hit removes a player, but only if that player is exposed. D wins if every O player is gone after five ticks, and the fewer D players it needs, the better.

Who is shielded

The formation is split into tetrahedra with as few pieces as possible. A player is shielded when it sits inside the formation and every tetrahedron it belongs to has all six edges shorter than 4 units. Players on the outer surface are always exposed. So D has to peel the formation from the outside in, and it has to read the triangulation correctly to know who can be hit right now.

    What each setting does

    Every rule of the game is a number you can change. The learned D was trained with one value of each, so values far from it test how well its skills carry over. Each chart below plays the same 40 test formations (10 each of 6, 10, 14 and 18 players) with only that setting changed.

    What it learned, and nothing else

    During training, a planner searched thousands of futures to find good moves, and the networks learned from it by imitation and then by trial and error. At play time the planner is gone. The learned D uses four networks and one matching step:

    Triangulation agent
    Starts from the Delaunay split and chooses flips one at a time until it stops, aiming for the fewest tetrahedra. Its result decides who D believes is exposed.
    Team-size network
    Estimates the chance of winning with 1, 2, 3 … players and takes the smallest number that reaches 90%.
    Placement network
    Scores candidate spots (players, midpoints, triangle and tetrahedron centres) and picks them one by one, preferring spots that cover players not yet covered.
    Per-tick network
    Moves every D player each tick and scores which reachable, exposed O player each one should hit. D players whose reach overlaps then share out the targets so no two hit the same player.

      Limits worth knowing

      • It is not the planner. At the planner's own team size the learned D wins 70% of test games, against the planner's 100% (the team size is defined as the smallest one where the planner wins all five games).
      • Big formations are harder. Wins at its own team size fall from about 85% for 4 to 6 players to about 63% for 20, because a big formation leaves no room for one missed hit.
      • It sometimes takes too few players. For 20-player formations it chose fewer players than the planner needed a third of the time, and those games are mostly lost.
      • A known input mismatch. When choosing targets, the per-tick network is told that nobody moved last tick, although it was trained with the real moves. The networks were fine-tuned with this in place, and a fix made no measurable difference on validation. The builder lets you switch the fix on.
      • Settings outside training. The networks never saw other speeds, radii or deadlines. The settings section shows how far their skills carry.

      How this page was made

      The game rules, the referee and the four trained networks were rewritten in JavaScript, so everything runs in your browser and no server is involved. On 119 test formations the browser version made the same team-size choice, the same placement and the same move and hit in every tick as the original Python and PyTorch code.

      The browser referee finds the fewest-tetrahedra triangulation by annealing. At full strength it matched the test referee's count on all 34 formations checked. Above 12 players a lighter setting is faster but sometimes settles one or two tetrahedra above the best.

      Weights: final models of the project (per-tick network from reinforcement learning at 20,400 games). Test-set numbers come from the project's final evaluation: 850 formations, five games each.

      Simplex game: a semester-end project in geometry and machine learning. Three.js is used for the 3D views (MIT licence).
