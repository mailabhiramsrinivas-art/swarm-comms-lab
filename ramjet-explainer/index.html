How a ramjet works

A ramjet is a tube that uses its own forward speed to squeeze the incoming air. Fuel is burned in that compressed air, and the hot gas leaves the back faster than the air came in. There is no compressor, no turbine and nothing that spins. Move the controls below to see how each part of the engine responds.

The four parts of the engine

Engineers number positions along an engine as stations. Here 0 is the undisturbed air ahead, 2 is the start of the combustor, 4 is the end of the combustor and 9 is the nozzle exit. Click a card to highlight that part in the cutaway.

1 · Intake

Catch the air

Above Mach 1 the air can't "hear" the engine coming, so it doesn't move aside smoothly. Instead it is slowed abruptly by shock waves. Each shock raises the pressure but also wastes some of the air's energy.

A single strong shock (pitot intake) wastes a lot. A pointed spike splits the job into a weaker angled shock plus a final normal shock, which wastes much less.

2 · Diffuser

Slow it down

Behind the shocks, the air is subsonic. The duct now widens, and in subsonic flow a widening duct slows the air further and raises its pressure.

By the combustor the air moves at only about a quarter of the local speed of sound. It is now compressed and has been heated by the compression alone.

3 · Combustor

Add heat

Fuel is sprayed in and burned. A flame in a fast stream blows out, much like a match in a gale, so flame holders are placed in the flow. These are V-shaped gutters that leave a sheltered pocket of slow, swirling gas behind them where the flame can anchor.

The gas leaves several times hotter, and so takes up several times more volume, and it speeds up.

4 · Nozzle

Turn heat into speed

The duct narrows to a throat, where the gas reaches exactly Mach 1, then widens again so the gas keeps accelerating as its pressure drops. This is a converging–diverging nozzle.

Thrust is the difference between the momentum leaving and the momentum entering. The hot jet leaves much faster than the air arrived.

Where it works, and where it doesn't

A ramjet only works within a range of speeds

Too slow: no squeeze

All of a ramjet's compression comes from its own speed. Standing still, the air inside is at the same pressure as the air outside, so burning fuel just makes hot gas that has no pressure to push it out fast. The engine makes no thrust when it isn't moving.

The ram pressure rises steeply with speed. At Mach 1 the air can be squeezed to about 1.9 times outside pressure. At Mach 3 the ideal figure is about 37 times. That is why ramjets usually come to life somewhere above Mach 1, and why every ramjet needs something else to get it moving. Historically, research ramjet aircraft were carried aloft by a mothership or were paired with a turbojet.

Too fast: too hot to burn

Slowing air down heats it. At Mach 5 the air reaching the combustor is already over 1,200 K before any fuel is burned. At Mach 6 it is close to 1,800 K. There is less and less room to add heat before the gas hits material and chemistry limits, so thrust per kilogram of air falls.

The answer is to stop slowing the air to subsonic speed and burn the fuel in a supersonic stream. That engine is a scramjet (supersonic-combustion ramjet), and it is a much harder problem.

The intake is the whole game

Why more shocks are better than one

Every shock wave turns some of the air's useful pressure into wasted heat. The loss rises steeply with the Mach number the shock sees. One shock at Mach 3 keeps only about a third of the available pressure. Two weaker shocks that each see a lower Mach number lose far less in total.

That's why fast intakes use a cone or a ramp: each surface bend creates a weaker angled shock. Good designs arrange for these shocks to meet right at the intake lip so no air spills around it. The best designs approach the ideal of an almost continuous, gentle compression.

The spike in this model is designed for one Mach number. Away from it, the angled shock either falls inside the lip or swings out ahead of it, and below a certain Mach number it detaches into a curved bow shock.

A short history

Old idea, hard engineering

  • 1913

    René Lorin, a French engineer, describes the ramjet principle. The aircraft of his day were far too slow for it to work.

  • 1949

    Leduc 0.10. René Leduc's research aircraft, carried up on top of a larger mothership and released, makes one of the first flights powered by a ramjet. Its pilot sat inside the engine's central body.

  • 1959

    Nord 1500 Griffon II. This French research aircraft combines a turbojet for take-off with a large ramjet around it for high speed. It sets a speed record over a 100 km closed course.

  • 1960s

    Combined-cycle engines. High-speed aircraft engines are designed so that at top speed much of the air bypasses the turbine machinery, and the engine behaves partly like a ramjet.

  • 2000s

    Scramjet research flights such as NASA's X-43A show that supersonic combustion can produce thrust in flight above Mach 6.

The model behind the numbers

This is a standard one-dimensional textbook cycle analysis, the kind used in a first propulsion course. It treats the flow as uniform at each station and uses fixed loss factors. It is good for understanding trends and rough magnitudes. It is not a design tool.

// Atmosphere: International Standard Atmosphere, 0–32 km
V₀ = M₀·√(γRT₀)    Tt0 = T₀(1 + ½(γ−1)M₀²)    pt0 = p₀(1 + ½(γ−1)M₀²)γ/(γ−1)
// Intake: shock losses × 0.97 subsonic diffuser loss
pt2 = pt0 · πshocks · 0.97    (pitot: one normal shock · spike: 16.7° oblique + normal · multi: MIL-E-5008B curve)
// Combustor: energy balance, 98% burning efficiency, 5% pressure loss
f = (cp,tTt4 − cp,cTt2) / (ηbh − cp,tTt4)    h = 42.8 MJ/kg (kerosene-type), capped at stoichiometric f = 0.068
// Nozzle: 2% loss, expanded to ambient pressure
M₉ = √( 2/(γt−1) · [ (pt9/p₀)(γt−1)/γt − 1 ] )    V₉ = M₉·√(γtRtT₉)
// Performance
F/ṁ₀ = (1+f)V₉ − V₀    Isp = (F/ṁ₀) / (f·g₀)    ηoverall = V₀·(F/ṁ₀) / (f·h)

Cold air: γ = 1.40, cp = 1004.5 J/kg·K. Hot gas: γ = 1.30, cp = 1239 J/kg·K. The combustor is entered at Mach 0.25. Station 4 speed is estimated from mass conservation in a constant-area burner. The nozzle drawn is the ideal shape for the current condition; a real fixed-geometry nozzle would only match this at one point.

Words used here

Glossary

Mach number
Speed divided by the local speed of sound. The speed of sound falls with temperature, so Mach 1 is about 340 m/s at sea level and 295 m/s at 11–20 km.
Static vs total pressure
Static pressure is what you'd feel moving with the air. Total pressure is what you'd get if you brought the air to rest without losses. It is the "squeeze budget" the intake has to work with.
Pressure recovery
The fraction of total pressure that survives the intake. Every percent lost is thrust lost.
Shock wave
A very thin layer where supersonic flow abruptly slows, compresses and heats. A normal shock stands straight across the flow; an oblique shock is angled and weaker.
Flame holder
A blunt object, often V-shaped, that leaves a sheltered wake where the flame can stay lit in a fast stream.
Throat
The narrowest point of the nozzle. When the nozzle is "choked", the gas there travels at exactly Mach 1.
Fuel–air ratio
Mass of fuel per mass of air. Stoichiometric (about 6.8% for kerosene) burns all the oxygen; beyond it extra fuel can't burn.
Specific impulse
Thrust divided by the weight of fuel burned per second, in seconds. It measures how efficiently an engine uses its fuel.
Scramjet
A ramjet in which the air stays supersonic through the combustor, used at speeds where slowing the air would make it too hot.
